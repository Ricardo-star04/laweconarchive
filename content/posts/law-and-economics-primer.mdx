## The Research Question

Law and economics asks how legal rules and institutions affect behavior, resource use, information production, and the allocation of risk. Its subject is not limited to prices or markets. Courts, firms, regulators, contracts, property rights, procedural rules, and informal norms are alternative governance arrangements. Each arrangement assigns decision rights, changes incentives, and incurs its own information and enforcement costs.

The useful research question is therefore comparative: **which feasible institution is likely to address a defined legal problem, for identified actors, under specified informational and enforcement conditions?** This formulation avoids two common errors. The first is to compare an imperfect legal institution with a frictionless market. The second is to compare an imperfect market with an omniscient and costless regulator. Neither comparison supplies a credible institutional baseline.

## Positive and Normative Analysis

### Positive claims

A positive analysis predicts or explains behavior. It may ask whether a disclosure duty changes information production, whether damages affect precaution, or whether voting rights alter managerial accountability. Such claims depend on assumptions about preferences, information, enforcement probability, legal salience, and available substitutes. The assumptions should be stated rather than hidden behind the general assertion that “incentives matter.”

### Normative claims

A normative analysis evaluates outcomes. Welfare analysis is one evaluative framework, but it does not eliminate questions about distribution, legality, procedural fairness, dignity, democratic authorization, or the protection of rights. Even within welfare analysis, researchers must identify whose welfare counts, how uncertainty and risk are treated, and whether willingness to pay is an acceptable measure when resources are unequally distributed.

Moving from a positive claim to a normative conclusion requires an additional argument. Evidence that a rule deters conduct does not by itself show that the rule is desirable: deterrence benefits must be compared with compliance costs, error costs, chilling effects, enforcement expenditures, and distributional consequences.

## Core Analytical Units

### Incentives and margins of response

Legal rules change expected benefits and costs, but actors may respond along several margins. A higher sanction may reduce misconduct, encourage concealment, shift activity into another legal form, or increase investment in legal advice. Governance rules may improve monitoring while also promoting defensive documentation. The analysis should identify the relevant actor, the decision margin, and the expected direction of response.

### Information

Many legal problems involve asymmetric information or costly verification. Securities disclosure, fiduciary duties, gatekeeper liability, consumer protection, and prudential supervision all influence who must produce information, who can verify it, and who bears the loss when information is inaccurate. More disclosure is not automatically better: standardization, complexity, timing, verification costs, and users’ capacity to process information affect its value.

### Transaction costs and entitlement design

Coase’s analysis does not imply that law becomes irrelevant [1]. It directs attention to the costs of identifying parties, defining rights, bargaining, monitoring performance, and enforcing agreements. When these costs are material, the initial assignment and mode of protection of an entitlement can affect both bargaining and ultimate resource use. Calabresi and Melamed’s distinction among property rules, liability rules, and inalienability rules provides one way to compare the consequences of consent, collectively set prices, and restrictions on transfer [2].

### Externalities and collective action

An externality is not merely an undesirable effect. It is a cost or benefit that an actor does not fully take into account. Legal analysis must identify why private ordering does not internalize the effect: parties may be numerous, future victims may be unidentified, information may be private, or strategic bargaining may block agreement. Regulation, taxation, liability, property rights, and collective governance are possible responses, each with different administrative and distributional consequences.

### Enforcement and error

A legal entitlement has practical force only through some combination of voluntary compliance, reputation, private enforcement, administrative supervision, and adjudication. Enforcement design affects detection, proof, settlement, remedies, and access to institutions. False positives and false negatives matter because they alter both the direct allocation of liability and the ex ante behavior of lawful and unlawful actors.

## Comparative Institutional Analysis

Williamson’s transaction-cost approach treats governance choices as comparisons among imperfect institutional forms [4]. For legal research, that insight suggests a disciplined sequence:

1. Define the institutional problem and the relevant legal relations.
2. Identify the actors, their information, and the decisions they can change.
3. State the market, organizational, regulatory, or judicial failure being alleged.
4. Specify the available institutional alternatives and their implementation costs.
5. Compare effects on incentives, information, risk allocation, enforcement, and distribution.
6. State what the analysis cannot resolve without an independent normative judgment.

This sequence also disciplines comparative-law work. A rule cannot be evaluated solely by its doctrinal label. Its effects depend on ownership structures, procedural access, judicial capacity, regulatory resources, professional intermediaries, and complementary institutions.

## From Legal Question to Research Design

A doctrinal project may clarify the content and hierarchy of rules before evaluating their effects. A comparative project may examine how different institutions perform the same governance function. An empirical project may measure description, association, prediction, or causation. The method should follow the claim rather than precede it.

For example, a researcher studying a new disclosure rule should first determine the legal duty, covered population, effective date, enforcement mechanism, and plausible behavioral channel. Only then can the researcher decide whether interviews, document coding, event studies, difference-in-differences, or another design can answer the question. A technically sophisticated estimate cannot repair a legally misclassified treatment or an implausible counterfactual.

## Limits and Responsible Use

Economic analysis is strongest when it makes tradeoffs and institutional assumptions explicit. It is weakest when efficiency vocabulary substitutes for evidence or when a stylized model is presented as a complete account of legal authority. Rights, procedure, legitimacy, and distribution may constrain otherwise efficient arrangements. Institutional actors may also lack the information or authority needed to implement a theoretically attractive rule.

The responsible conclusion is conditional: given specified actors, frictions, evidence, and institutional alternatives, one arrangement may perform better along identified dimensions. That conclusion should remain open to revision when the legal setting, enforcement capacity, or empirical record changes.
